My trip to Japan

My background: Hi, my name is Joseph, and I'm currently a student at Brigham Young University–Idaho, majoring in Accounting. Though I was born and raised in the United States, my roots are Korean, and that heritage has always been a big part of who I am — from the food I grew up eating to the music and culture I carry with me. Outside of school, I have a few passions that keep life interesting. I'm a big anime fan — whether it's a slow-burn psychological thriller or an action-packed shonen series, I'm always in the middle of something. Traveling is another love of mine; there's something about stepping into a completely different environment that resets my perspective and reminds me how wide the world actually is. And when I'm not studying or exploring, you'll probably find me grinding ranked matches in CS:GO — I take my aim training pretty seriously. I'm someone who enjoys connecting with people from different backgrounds, which probably comes naturally given my own mix of cultures. I'm always happy to chat about anything from academics to the best anime of the season. Nice to meet you!

Why I love Japan: Honestly, my love for Japan started with anime. Long before I ever thought about visiting, I was already living in imaginary versions of Tokyo through shows and films — the rain-soaked streets, the convenience stores glowing at midnight, the quiet rooftops where characters go to think. Japan felt like a place I already knew, even before I knew it. Then came the food, and that sealed everything. Japanese cuisine isn't just delicious — it's intentional. Every dish feels like someone cared deeply about making it right. A bowl of ramen that's been simmered for twelve hours. Sushi where the rice temperature matters as much as the fish. Takoyaki eaten straight off the griddle at a street stall in Osaka. Even a simple convenience store onigiri somehow tastes better than it has any right to. There's a level of craft and attention in Japanese food culture that I genuinely respect. But beyond the anime and the food, what draws me to Japan is its sense of balance. It's a country that built bullet trains and robot restaurants, yet still maintains century-old temples in the middle of busy cities. You can stand at Shibuya Crossing, one of the busiest intersections on the planet, and then walk twenty minutes to a moss-covered shrine that feels completely removed from the noise. That contrast — modern and ancient, loud and quiet, chaotic and ordered — is something I find endlessly fascinating. Japan feels like a place with its own soul. And once it gets you, it doesn't really let go.
